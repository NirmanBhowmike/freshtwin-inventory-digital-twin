# FreshTwin v0.5 — suggested three-minute narration

**Use this as a rehearsal aid, not as a script to submit unchanged.** The assignment specifies that the discussion should be written by the student. Verify values displayed by the live application before recording and explain decisions in your own words.

**0:00–0:30 — Problem.** Fresh food inventory is difficult because purchases, demand, expiration and shelf space interact. A store can appear to have low demand when a product was actually out of stock. Ordering more may improve service but can create waste, especially for vegetables with short shelf lives. My project is FreshTwin, a simulation and decision-support application for studying these trade-offs.

**0:30–1:00 — Evidence and assumptions.** The research benchmark uses normalized sales and stockout observations from FreshRetailNet-50K. The real product IDs are anonymous, so I have not assigned them vegetable names. The new joint lab instead uses ten transparently synthetic vegetables to represent one hypothetical grocery store. Their shelf lives, costs and inventory levels are assumptions that can be changed; they are not measured retailer records.

**1:00–1:35 — Model extension.** The basic problem is deciding an order quantity for one item. I extended it to ten vegetables competing for one purchasing budget and one refrigerated-storage limit. Each product has its own lead time and shelf life. My model follows first-expiring-first-out inventory movement and rejects orders that would violate either shared restriction. The budget applies to the whole planning horizon, not one day.

**1:35–2:10 — Computational methods.** I compare a sales-based reorder rule, a stockout-adjusted reorder rule, and a coupled heuristic that evaluates forward demand scenarios and allocates units based on their marginal cost benefit. I use the same random demand path for all three policies in each trial. This makes the comparison more meaningful than running each policy under unrelated future conditions. The model reports service rate, expiration, modeled cost and estimated tail risk.

**2:10–2:40 — Interpret actual output.** For the default 21-day experiment, the coupled method increases mean fill rate compared with the recorded-sales rule, but it also costs more and expires more units. I would not call this an automatic economic improvement. It demonstrates that a policy can improve availability while worsening waste or cost. Changing the budget or storage level reveals which constraint drives the decision. The daily ledger allows me to reconcile every receipt, sale, expired unit and closing balance.

**2:40–3:05 — Engineering verification.** AI helped me develop the prototype, but I treated verification as a separate engineering task. The repository has deterministic regression tests, a small-instance exhaustive search oracle, and real-browser acceptance tests. The coupled allocator is approximate, not a certified global optimum. The results are generated by a synthetic store, so future work would require measured costs, age-cohort observations and independent field validation.

**References to mention if appropriate:** Wang et al. (2025), *FreshRetailNet-50K*, arXiv:2505.16319; Sharma, Abouee-Mehrizi and Sartor (2020), *Inventory Management under Storage and Order Restrictions*, *Production and Operations Management*, DOI 10.1111/poms.13097.
